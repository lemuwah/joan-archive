The Merchant Bonaventure was an English merchant vessel, not Dutch.  The primary ship details from the port records are:Ship Name: Merchant Bonaventure (sometimes transcribed as Merch: Bonaventure or Merchant Bonadvanture).  Port of Departure: Port of London (Gravesend).Destination: Virginia Colony (though some port records list an initial stop or routing through St. Domingo / St. Christopher).Master (Captain): James Ricroft (often transcribed as James Ricrofte or James Riccoste in the original London port licenses).  Departure Date: Early January 1634/35.  Where does "Dutch" or "Atherton" come from?Not Dutch: The vessel was operating out of London under English authority, carrying roughly 150 English passengers who were required to take the Oath of Allegiance and Supremacy to the English Crown before the magistrates at Gravesend before departing.  "Atherton": There is no Master, owner, or primary factor named Atherton associated with the 1634/35 Merchant Bonaventure voyage in the London port rolls. It is possible "Atherton" is being confused with:Humphrey Atherton, a prominent 17th-century New England Puritan leader/colonist (though he sailed later, around 1636, to Massachusetts, not on this Virginia voyage).A different vessel or privateer altogether (as "Bonaventure" was a very common ship name in the 1600s, shared by several English and naval vessels of the era).

If a figure named John (or any migrant of that era) had sailed on the Merchant Bonaventure in 1634/35, it would place him directly in the wave of early Chesapeake tobacco labor, transatlantic trade, and indentured servitude.1. What It Would Suggest About His HistoryIndentured Servitude / Headright Labor: Over 80% of young men traveling to Virginia in the 1630s were indentured servants who sold 4 to 7 years of their labor in exchange for passage across the Atlantic. If an adult entered Virginia via this ship without significant independent wealth or a royal grant, he likely served an initial indenture in the Chesapeake before gaining freedom, moving north, or acquiring land.Chesapeake-First Pattern: Many early 17th-century New England and Long Island Sound settlers actually started in the Chesapeake (Virginia or Maryland) during the tobacco boom of the 1630s. If someone arrived via the Merchant Bonaventure to Virginia and later resurfaced in Rhode Island, Connecticut, or New York, it would indicate a common migration pattern: working off an indenture or trade term in Virginia before relocating north via coastal trading sloops.  Young, Unattached Laborer Demographic: The manifest of the Merchant Bonaventure is heavily skewed toward young, working-age males (mostly ages 16–25). It was not a "Puritan family migration" ship like those bound for Massachusetts Bay (e.g., the Winthrop Fleet or the Planter). Passengers were heading into the harsh, tobacco-driven frontier of the Tidewater.  2. Modus Operandi of the Merchant BonaventureThe Merchant Bonaventure was a quintessential 1630s English transatlantic merchant vessel operating on the triangular trade routes between London, the West Indies, and the Chesapeake.  A. The Outward Bound Trip (Human Cargo & Supplies)Outfitting in London: Ships like the Bonaventure loaded passengers at the Port of London (Gravesend) after swearing required oaths before King Charles I’s port officials.The Transit Route: They frequently sailed south through the Atlantic trade winds to the Caribbean (often stopping at St. Christopher, Nevis, or St. Domingo) to resupply, trade goods, or load salt before making landfall in the Chesapeake Bay (New Point Comfort / Jamestown / Rappahannock area).  Bulk Passenger Bulk Freight: The ship packed roughly 150+ young passengers into extremely tight steerage conditions. Many of these passages were financed by English merchants, "factors" (trade agents), or plantation owners looking to claim headrights (50 acres of free Virginia land per imported person).B. The Return Trip (The Tobacco Trade)Monopolizing Tobacco: Upon reaching Virginia, the captain (James Ricroft) unloaded passengers and imported manufactured English goods (tools, cloth, ironware).Loading Bulk Leaf: The Bonaventure was then filled to the gunwales with hogsheads of Virginia tobacco and raw timber for the return voyage to London.Merchant Syndicates: Vessels of this class operated on commission for London-based merchant syndicates (such as the Virginia Company veterans or private London tobacco merchants) who coordinated with Virginia planters to handle cargo on both ends.Summary of the Vessel's ProfileThe Merchant Bonaventure was an economic engine for the London-Chesapeake tobacco trade. Unlike the religious pioneer ships carrying intact Puritan families to Boston or Plymouth, the Bonaventure was a commercial transport moving young laborers to tobacco plantations in exchange for cash crops and headright land credits.
