## Part 1: Joan's Lifetime (1640s–1690s)
### Why It Was Dangerous to Be Identified as Indigenous

The following is the legal environment in which Joan lived, signed deeds, and disappeared from the record. Each entry is verified against published primary sources or peer-reviewed scholarship.

### 1.1 The Disposal System (1676)

**Source:** *Early Records of the Town of Providence*, Vol. 15, pp. 151–154. Originals at RIHS. Also confirmed by Linford Fisher (Brown University) in peer-reviewed scholarship.

Rhode Island's March 13, 1676 law declared that no Indian should be enslaved except to pay debts, compensate custodians, or fulfill prior covenants. Despite this formal language, RI magistrates appointed committees to "set the disposal" of Indians. In practice, this was **limited-term enslavement disguised as servitude.**

At a Providence town meeting on August 14–16, 1676, officials set graduated age-based terms:
- Under 5: served until age 30
- Ages 5–10: served until age 28
- Ages 11–15: served until age 27
- Ages 16–20: served until age 26
- Ages 21–30: 8 years
- Over 30: 7 years

Shares of the "product" of Indigenous people were distributed among prominent men — including Roger Williams. This was revenue extraction, not guardianship.

### 1.2 The Certificate System (October 1676)

**Source:** RI State Archives catalog records.

RI ordered that any Indigenous person who could not produce a certificate authorizing their presence could be "disposed for the benefit of the Collony." The certificate placed the **burden of proof on the Indigenous person** — no paper meant seizure.

This created a documentary regime where being identified as Indigenous without English-issued credentials was itself a legal vulnerability.

### 1.3 Women and Children Especially Vulnerable

**Source:** Fisher (Brown), peer-reviewed; Peter Freeman petition (1685).

Men were executed or shipped overseas for sale. Women and children were distributed among English households. The 1685 petition of Peter Freeman documents his daughter being "taken and made a slave" — family separation lasting years.

### 1.4 The Quidnessett Corridor

**Source:** Late 1679 / early 1680 petition (published in RI colonial records).

The petition references "great quantities of conquered Land" and money from selling "many thousands of Indians." Quidnessett and Wickford sat at the intersection of Indigenous communities, English trading posts, and mixed social networks — exactly where the documentary pressure was most intense.

### 1.5 English Names as Survival Strategy

**Source:** Fisher (Brown), peer-reviewed; multiple examples in colonial records.

Adopting English/Christian names made a person more legible to courts, churches, and employers. This was pragmatic and limited — not evidence that Indigenous identity disappeared. People continued to assert tribal and kinship identities. Both names were often preserved in records (e.g., Peter Freeman still identified as Narragansett; Wenepoykin also "Sagamore George").

**⚠️ This means:** An Indigenous woman who adopted an English name in the 1680s was not betraying her identity — she was protecting her family's legal standing. The absence of a Native name in the English record does not prove English origin.

### 1.6 The Five Officials and the Atherton Company

The following RI officials held office during the 1670s–1680s and opposed the Atherton Company's private land purchases using Connecticut authority. They had institutional incentive to control which land titles were recognized — and that control mechanism ran through the clerks who recorded deeds.

**All verified against published RI colonial records:**

- **John Sanford** — General Recorder of RI, 1678–1686. Opposed Atherton/Connecticut claim. ✅
- **John Clarke** — Colonial agent, procured the 1663 charter. Leading defender of RI's title. ✅ (died 1676)
- **Henry Bull** — Deputy for Newport 1680, Governor 1685–1686. Defended charter government. ✅
- **John Easton** — Deputy Governor 1674–1676, Governor 1690–1695. Supported RI institutional defense. ✅
- **Walter Todd** — Deputy for Warwick, 1678. Supported regulation of competing land titles. ✅

**⚠️ This means:** The recording of deeds in the Narragansett corridor was not a neutral administrative act. Clerks had political stakes in which titles were preserved, which were suppressed, and how signatories were described. This does not prove suppression of Joan's identity — but it establishes that the recording environment was politically charged.

## Part 2: La Mance's Era (1890s–1904)
### Why It Was Still Dangerous to Have Mixed Pedigree

When Thomas La Mance published his Greene genealogy in 1904, identifying a colonial ancestor's wife as Indigenous carried real social consequences.

### 2.1 Anti-Miscegenation Ideology

**Source:** Massachusetts 1786 statute ("An Act for the orderly Solemnization of Marriages") — prohibited ministers from marrying "any white person with any Negro, Indian or Mulatto" and declared such marriages null.

Although repealed before the 1890s, the ideology persisted in social institutions. A documented Native mother was far more disruptive to a family's social standing than a distant "Indian princess" legend — because it connected ancestry to **living Indigenous communities** with ongoing legal and social claims.

### 2.2 Blood Quantum and Racial Classification

**Source:** Bureau of Indian Affairs census rolls (1890s onward).

The BIA was actively using census rolls to assign blood degrees by the 1890s. Blood quantum operated as both a gate to tribal rights and a social stigma — it worked in both directions. Being classified as "mixed" could cost a family access to white social institutions (hereditary societies, property covenants) while also being insufficient for tribal enrollment.

### 2.3 Dawes Act Effects on Eastern Communities

**Source:** Dawes Act (1887), published scholarship on Eastern tribal identity.

**Important caveat:** The Dawes Commission primarily concerned the Five Tribes in Indian Territory. **Eastern communities — Wampanoag, Narragansett, Mohegan, Pequot, Abenaki — faced different state and local regimes.** New England families could have genuine Indigenous ancestry without appearing on any Dawes Roll.

This is exactly the situation the Joan Archive documents — the absence from rolls does not mean absence from bloodlines.

### 2.4 Genealogical Societies as Racial Gatekeeping

**Source:** Published histories of hereditary societies (DAR, SAR, Mayflower Society).

Hereditary societies required lineal descent from approved colonial ancestors. Indigenous ancestry was simultaneously romanticized as colonial past and stigmatized as racial mixture. La Mance writing in 1904 was operating inside this framework — where "Joan Beggarly" (a safely English-sounding name with no Indigenous connection) was preferable to "Joan [unknown]" or "Joan [possibly Narragansett]."

**⚠️ This means:** La Mance may not have been lying maliciously. He may have been applying the standard genealogical practice of his era: when a wife's identity was unknown, assign her the most respectable available English name. That the name he chose (Alice Beggarly) belongs to a woman who died 38 years before Joan appeared is evidence of carelessness or willful substitution — not necessarily conspiracy. But the effect is the same: it erased the question.

## What This Framework Does and Does Not Prove

**It proves:**
- The legal environment in Joan's lifetime made Indigenous identity dangerous to acknowledge in English records
- The social environment in La Mance's lifetime made Indigenous ancestry dangerous to acknowledge in genealogies
- The recording of deeds in the Narragansett corridor was politically charged, not neutral
- The documentary silence around Joan is consistent with either English or Indigenous identity — **the silence itself is not evidence for either**

**It does not prove:**
- That Joan was Indigenous
- That her record was deliberately suppressed
- That La Mance knew she was Indigenous and covered it up
- That any specific clerk altered any specific deed

**The framework explains why the record looks the way it does. It does not tell us who Joan was. That question remains open.**
