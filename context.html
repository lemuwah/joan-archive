Historical Context

The world around Joan's single documented appearance — presented without synthesis to her identity.

This page provides the colonial and Indigenous historical setting for the 1682 deed. It describes the place, the people documented nearby, and the legal and political landscape. It does not connect any of this context to Joan's identity. That work belongs on the Analysis page, where each competing model is tested against the evidence.

Quidnessett and the Narragansett Country

Quidnessett (also spelled Quithnesset, Quidnesset) was located in what is now North Kingstown, Rhode Island, on the western shore of Narragansett Bay. In the 1670s–1680s, it sat within contested territory claimed by multiple colonial jurisdictions and by Narragansett leaders.

The area included Cocumscussoc (also Cocumscussett), the site of Richard Smith's trading post, which served as a point of English–Indigenous interaction from the 1630s onward.

Sources: Chapin (1931), Potter (1835), RI Colonial Records.

John Greene of Quidnessett — Documented Interactions

The following are documented facts about Joan's husband. Each is cited to a specific source. No characterization ("embedded," "allied," "trusted") is applied.

  • 1679 affidavit: John Greene stated he had lived in Narragansett Country "forty years and more." (Bates, RIHS Collections)
  • 1676: John Greene transported the sachem Potuck by sloop for peace negotiations. (RI Colonial Records)
  • 1682 deed (March): John Greene conveyed land at Quidnessett to sons Daniel and James. Joan is named as his wife. A 30-shilling annuity is reserved to her. (Worth abstract, RI Land Evidences Vol. I, p. 122)
  • 1672 deed (Fones Record): John Greene was party to a land transaction involving Narragansett land. The deed was executed in 1672 and enrolled in 1703. (Fones Record, RIHS)

Note: Earlier versions of this archive described John Greene as "embedded in Narragansett governance." That is a characterization, not a documented fact. The documented interactions are listed above. The reader may draw their own conclusions. See inference_audit.md #13.

Narragansett Political Structure

📖 EXTERNAL INTERPRETATION — derived from Chapin (1931), Potter (1835), and colonial correspondence. Not verified by Narragansett authority.

Colonial-era English sources describe a Narragansett leadership structure involving multiple sachems with overlapping territorial and kin-based authority. The following names appear in the documentary record for the Quidnessett / Narragansett region:

  • Scuttup — described as a sachem; death recorded c. 1668. (Potter 1835)
  • Quaiapin — described as a female sachem; active during King Philip's War. Potuck served as counselor. (Chapin 1931)
  • Anashuecot / Awashouse — name(s) appearing in land transactions involving Narragansett territory. Whether these refer to one person or two is ⚫ UNVERIFIED — an open question. (Potter 1835, Fones Record)

This archive defers to Narragansett authority on their own governance, kinship, and political structures. The descriptions above reflect what English colonial sources recorded, which is inherently limited and shaped by colonial priorities. See Epistemology.md.

King Philip's War / Metacom's War (1675–1678)

The conflict known in English colonial sources as "King Philip's War" is named for the Wampanoag leader Metacom (called "King Philip" by the English). Narragansett and other Indigenous nations use their own names for this conflict and its constituent events. This archive uses the colonial name for documentary reference while acknowledging that naming carries framing.

The war devastated the Narragansett Country. Documented events relevant to the archive's geographic scope:

  • December 1675: The Great Swamp Fight. Colonial forces attacked a Narragansett fortification. (Multiple published histories)
  • 1676: The Cocumscussoc trading post was burned. (Citation needed — see inference_audit.md #9)
  • Post-1676: Rhode Island passed legislation authorizing disposal of captured Indigenous persons, including graduated service terms for children. (Early Records of the Town of Providence, Vol. 15, pp. 151–154)

Note: Whether John Greene's homestead survived the war is not documented. He partitioned the same land in 1682. See inference_audit.md #1.

Colonial Legal Framework for Women and Land

Under 17th-century English colonial law in Rhode Island:

  • Married women generally could not hold property independently (coverture).
  • A wife's dower right — typically one-third of the husband's estate for her lifetime — required her consent to extinguish when land was conveyed.
  • Whether Joan was asked to consent to the 1682 deed is ⚫ SUSPENDED — the sources we hold do not describe her consenting. See suspended_items.md S-002.
  • The 30-shilling annuity may represent a negotiated alternative to dower, but this is inference. See Analysis.
