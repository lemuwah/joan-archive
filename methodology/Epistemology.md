 Source Epistemology Note 
Source Epistemology: How This Archive Reads Colonial Records

The documentary record for 17th‑century Rhode Island is overwhelmingly colonial in origin. Deeds, probate files, court records, and town books were created to document English property, English inheritance, and English governance. As a result, these sources reflect the priorities, categories, and worldview of the colonists who produced them. They do not reliably record Indigenous identity, kinship, or social structure, and the absence of Indigenous women from these documents should not be interpreted as absence from history.

This archive therefore treats colonial records as partial instruments:

reliable for documenting land transactions, boundaries, and English legal procedure,

unreliable for documenting Indigenous identity, kinship, or social belonging.

Because of this structural bias, the absence of information about a woman’s origins, kin, or cultural identity in colonial documents cannot be taken as evidence that such identity did not exist. At the same time, the archive does not infer identity without primary sources. Instead, it holds multiple possibilities open until evidence supports or eliminates them.

This epistemological stance guides the treatment of Joan (Unknown) Greene’s ancestry. The archive recognizes both the limitations of the colonial record and the necessity of grounding conclusions in verifiable evidence.
