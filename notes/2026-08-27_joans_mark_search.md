## Alternative Paths to the Mark

With remote requests exhausted, these are the remaining realistic paths:

### Path 1: In-Person Visit (Highest Probability of Success)

**Option A — FamilySearch Center**
- Locate the nearest FamilySearch Center or Affiliate Library
- Request to view films **564389** (RI Land Evidences Vol. I, 1648–1696) and/or **22291–22292** (Peirce Manuscripts)
- FamilySearch Centers have microfilm readers and some allow you to photograph frames
- **Cost:** Free. **Barrier:** Travel to a center with these films or the ability to order them.

**Option B — RIHS Reading Room (Providence, RI)**
- The original Peirce Manuscripts are held at RIHS
- They declined remote scanning but **do allow in-person access by appointment**
- You could view the original manuscript pages and photograph Joan's mark yourself (subject to their photography policy)
- **Contact:** https://www.rihs.org/library/manuscripts/
- **Cost:** Free to visit; reproduction fees may apply.

**Option C — RI State Archives (Providence, RI)**
- If the original deed books are held here rather than RIHS, same logic applies: in-person access may be permitted even when remote scanning isn't.

### Path 2: Research Partnership / Proxy Researcher

- **NEHGS (New England Historic Genealogical Society)** offers research services for a fee. A targeted request (“Photograph one specific deed page”) would be affordable and they have institutional access.
- **University research partnerships** — If a graduate student or faculty member at URI, Brown, or Providence College has related research interests, they may be able to photograph the page as part of their own archive access.
- **Genealogical societies** — The Rhode Island Genealogical Society or local chapters sometimes fulfill specific lookup requests for members.

### Path 3: Grant-Funded Digitization

- The Joan Archive itself could be the basis for a digitization partnership proposal. Organizations like the **Council on Library and Information Resources (CLIR)** and **NEH Preservation & Access** fund exactly this kind of work — digitizing fragile colonial-era documents.
- This is a longer-term play but could result in the entire deed book being digitized, not just Joan's page.

### Path 4: Comparative Mark Analysis (What We Can Do Right Now)

Even without Joan's mark in hand, we can build the comparison framework:
- **Collect other digitized colonial women's marks** from the same period and region (1670s–1690s Rhode Island)
- **Catalog mark styles** — X marks, initials, pictographic marks, other symbols
- **Build a typology** so that when Joan's mark is eventually seen, it can be immediately placed in context
- **Cross-reference with the Brown "Their Marks" project data** (Indigenous marks from the same era) to understand the visual vocabulary of marks in that time and place — with full respect for the distinct cultural contexts

This comparative framework doesn't require Joan's mark to begin. It would make the eventual comparison far more meaningful.

## Why the Mark Still Matters

Joan's mark on the 19 May 1682 Pawtuxet deed is the **only physical evidence she personally created**. Every other record mentioning her was written by someone else. The mark is:
- The closest thing to Joan's own "voice" in the archive
- Potentially diagnostic: the shape, confidence, and style of the mark could indicate literacy level, cultural context, or comparison with other known marks
- A concrete visual artifact in a project that has been working almost entirely from text transcriptions
- Comparable: once found, it can be placed alongside marks from other colonial women AND (separately, with appropriate cultural framing) Indigenous marks from the Brown project

**The mark is not lost. It is inaccessible. Those are different problems with different solutions.**
