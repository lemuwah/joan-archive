## Purpose

The 24 March 1682 home-place deed includes the clause: *"or to her mother if she survive."* This clause is unusual. The purpose of this baseline study is to determine **how unusual** by measuring its frequency against comparable deeds.

**Why this matters:**
- If this clause appears regularly in 1680s Rhode Island deeds, it tells us nothing special about Joan's situation — it's just standard drafting.
- If it's rare or unique, it may indicate something specific about Joan's mother: that she was alive, nearby, known to the parties, and expected to potentially outlive Joan.
- The rarity or commonness of this clause directly affects the weight we can place on it as evidence.

## Methodology

### Sample Frame
- **Target:** All recorded land deeds in the Narragansett/Quidnessett region, 1670–1695
- **Secondary:** All recorded land deeds in Rhode Island colony, same period
- **Tertiary:** Comparable deeds in Plymouth, Connecticut, and Massachusetts colonies, same period

### What We're Looking For
- Any deed that names a wife's mother as contingent beneficiary
- Any deed that includes a "if she survive" survivorship clause for anyone other than the grantee or spouse
- Any deed that names a third-generation relative (grandparent) as contingent holder

### Sources to Check
- *Early Records of the Town of Providence,* Vols. 1–21 (Internet Archive)
- *Rhode Island Land Evidences,* Vol. I (1648–1696)
- *Records of the Colony of Rhode Island and Providence Plantations* (Bartlett ed.)
- Comparable deed books from neighboring colonies (as available digitized)

### Classification Scale
- **COMMON:** Found in >10% of sampled deeds
- **UNCOMMON:** Found in 2–10% of sampled deeds
- **RARE:** Found in <2% of sampled deeds
- **UNIQUE:** Found only in the Joan/John Greene 1682 deed

## Preliminary Observations (Pre-Research)

**These are NOT findings. They are questions to be tested.**

- The clause implies Joan's mother was **alive in March 1682** — this is a documented inference, not a documented fact, since the clause is conditional.
- The clause implies the drafters (and Joan and John) expected Joan's mother to be **geographically reachable** — a contingent beneficiary in England or an unknown location would be legally pointless.
- If unique, the clause may reflect a non-standard family structure — one where the wife's mother had an unusually prominent role or where standard inheritance patterns (to children, to husband's kin) didn't apply.

**Warning (Law 1 — No Smoothing):** We must resist the temptation to read the Anashuecot hypothesis into this clause. The clause is compatible with multiple explanations — English mother, Indigenous mother, widowed mother with no other support, elderly mother living in the household. All remain open until baseline data narrows the field.
