## Why This Matters

English clerks in 17th-century New England had no standardized system for transliterating Algonquian names. Each clerk wrote what they heard, and what they heard depended on their own phonetic biases, regional accent, and familiarity with Indigenous languages. The same person could be recorded under radically different spellings in different documents — or even within the same document.

**Every variant is a potential search key.** When searching archival catalogs, databases, and digitized collections, each spelling must be tried independently. A search for "Anashuecot" alone will miss records filed under "Awashouse" or "Awashowat."

### From Potter 1835 — *Early History of Narragansett* (VERIFIED Aug 28, 2026)

**Source:** Elisha R. Potter, *Early History of Narragansett* (1835). Read directly from Internet Archive PDF: https://archive.org/details/earlyhistoryofna00pott_0

The following variants appear in Potter's published text and appendix. Potter reproduced original deeds and testimonies, so these spellings reflect the original documents he had access to.

- **Awashowat** — p. 76 (printed page). Heading for the Jan 1, 1672 Fones Purchase deed: "Awashowat's deed to John Greene, Thomas Waterman, John Andrew, Henry Tibbits, John Briggs and John Fones." This is Potter's rendering of the grantor's name for the same deed recorded in the Fones Record as "Anashuecot."
- **Awashwash** — p. 243 (appendix). Witness mark on Sosoa's deed. "Awashwash ^ his mark."
- **Awashous** — pp. 243–244 (appendix). "The mark q of AWASHOUS" — witness to Cachaquant's testimony. Also: "0= Awashous witness to it" on Cachaquant/Sammecat declaration.
- **Awashouse** — pp. 245, 249, 267 (appendix). Signed declaration affirming Socho's land rights (June 15, 1661): "I the said Awashouse have hitherto sett my hand this present 15th day of June 1661. The marke of 0= AWASHOUSE." Also: Oct 5, 1661 deed for Nisquitianxsett: "we Awashouse and Nucom both of Narroganset being the owners of a certain tract of land called Nisquitianxsett." **★ This is Awashouse as a LAND-OWNING SIGNATORY, not just a witness.**
- **Awashshass** — p. 246 (appendix). Witness to Pessicus's confirmation of Socho's land. "Awash ^ shass."
- **Awashshous** — p. 249 (appendix). Witness mark on Wawaloam's affirmation: "The mark of P-i Awashshous."

### Key Discovery: Awashouse as Land Owner (Oct 5, 1661)

On p. 267 (appendix), Potter reproduces the October 5, 1661 deed in which **Awashouse and Nucom** sell Nisquitianxsett to William Vaughan and the Misquamicuck purchasers. This is not a witness role — Awashouse is a **principal grantor**, described as an "owner" of the land.

This is significant because:
1. It establishes Awashouse/Anashuecot as a person with documented land ownership rights
2. The date (1661) predates the 1672 Fones Purchase by 11 years
3. Awashouse appears across multiple deeds between 1660–1672 — a consistent presence in Narragansett land transactions
4. The same person is named alongside Nucom (interpreter) in multiple documents

**⚠️ Per Law 1:** The identification of Awashouse (1660–1661 documents) with Anashuecot (1672 Fones deed) is based on phonetic similarity, geographic overlap, and the continuity of the Nucom association. It is highly probable but not proven beyond doubt until a document explicitly links both names to the same individual.

## Tier 3: Related But Distinct Names (NOT Anashuecot)

These names share phonetic elements with Anashuecot but are documented as **separate persons or places**. They are listed here to prevent false conflation.

- The morpheme **-tucket** (river/stream) distinguishes it from **-cot** (personal name element)

- Variants: Anaquatucket, Anaquatocket, Annaquatucket, Annocotuckett, Annocottuckett, Annogatucket, Annocotucket, Anochetuckett, Orkatucket

## Phonetic Pattern Notes

The variants cluster around two phonetic openings:

1. **An-** cluster: Anashuecot, Anashuecut, Anashuecutt, Anashusett
2. **Aw-** cluster: Awashueset, Awashowat, Awashwash, Awashous, Awashouse, Awashshass, Awashshous

The An-/Aw- distinction likely reflects different scribal conventions. The Fones Record scribe used An-; Potter's appendix documents (from Newport and Warwick clerks) consistently use Aw-. The underlying Algonquian phoneme is probably the same.

**Per Law 1:** Phonetic similarity is not identity evidence. Two names that sound alike to English ears may be different Algonquian names, or the same name rendered by different scribes. We document both possibilities without collapsing them. However, the consistent association with Nucom (interpreter) and the geographic overlap across 1660–1672 documents strongly suggests a single individual.
