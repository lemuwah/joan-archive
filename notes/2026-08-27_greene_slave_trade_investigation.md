## Direct Evidence: Marcy and Sarah

John Greene held an Indian servant named **Marcy**. Marcy's daughter **Sarah** was born in Greene's house. Greene agreed to indenture Sarah as a servant or apprentice.

**Source:** Smith's Castle / Cocumscussoc Review research publications.

**What this tells us:**
- Greene's household included at least one Native woman in a service/labor capacity
- Greene exercised legal control over the placement of that woman's child
- The terms "indenture," "servant," and "apprentice" were legal categories that could function as euphemisms for coerced labor

**What this does NOT tell us (per Law 1):**
- Whether Marcy was captured during King Philip's War or entered service by other means
- Whether Marcy's service was voluntary, coerced, or enslaved
- Whether Sarah's indenture was a postwar war-captive disposition
- Any connection between Marcy and Joan or Anashuecot

**⚠️ CRITICAL OPEN QUESTION:** The full archival citation for the Marcy/Sarah indenture record has not yet been located. This needs to be found — the date, court, and specific terms would clarify whether this was a wartime captive situation.

## Structural Evidence: Greene's Embeddedness

John Greene was embedded in the colonial system that dispossessed and coerced Narragansett people:
- He arrived as an agent of Richard Smith's trading post at Cocumscussoc
- He acquired Narragansett land (c.1651)
- He lived in contested Narragansett territory for 40+ years
- His household included at least one Indian servant
- His neighbors and political associates (Smith, Atherton Company members) were directly involved in the post-King Philip's War captive economy

**This does not make Greene a slave trader.** It makes him a participant in a system. The distinction matters for accuracy, but the system itself must not be euphemized.

## The Regional Context: Post–King Philip's War Native Enslavement

### Scale
- Massachusetts sold approximately **190 Indians** (about two-thirds women, girls, or infants) at public auctions in August–September 1676
- Plymouth sold another large group; roughly **120 Plymouth Indians** were sent to the Caribbean
- Documented destinations: Bermuda, Barbados, Jamaica, Spain, Portugal, Madeira, the Azores, Cádiz

### Colonial Authorizations
- **Plymouth** required captured Indian males over fourteen to be sent outside the colony. Certificates described captives as *"sentenced and condemned to perpetual servitude"*
- **Massachusetts** authorized officials to *"dispose"* of surrendering Indians *"by shipping them off or otherwise"*
- **Rhode Island** used committees to *"set the disposal"* of Native people with graduated service terms by age
- **Connecticut** ordered surrendered Indians *"dissposed of for the benefit of the country"*

### Cocumscussoc Specifically
A later account describes Native people held in Providence being removed by vessel toward Cocumscussoc, where they were confined and exchanged for money, corn, wool, and livestock — including *"three fat sheep."* This links the Smith property to the regional captivity economy.

### Sachem Families Were Targeted
- **Canonchet** (Narragansett war sachem): captured April 1676, executed — shot, quartered, decapitated, head sent to Hartford
- **Quaiapen** ("Old Queen," Narragansett-Niantic female sachem): killed July 2, 1676 in ambush, along with ~138 people including women and children
- **Metacom's wife and young son** (Wampanoag): captured and **sold to Bermuda**

## "Conveyed Out of the Country" — The Absolom Affidavit Language

### The Text (1677/78)
Absolom's affidavit states that Anashuecot was *"conveyed out of the country."*

### Interpretation

**Most likely meaning: Exported as a captive** (HIGH probability)
- *"Conveyed"* was a transportation verb meaning carried, sent, or delivered elsewhere
- *"Out of the country"* identified the geographic result: removal beyond the colony
- This was the standard postwar vocabulary in records concerning Indian captives
- Colonial authorities expressly ordered captives sold outside the colony

**Possible but less likely: Forced exile/deportation without sale** (MODERATE probability)
- "Out of the country" could describe removal generally
- But the postwar policy context overwhelmingly involved sale, servitude, or forced relocation

**Very unlikely: Death/execution euphemism** (LOW probability)
- Colonial records generally stated that leaders were *"killed," "executed,"* or *"put to death"*
- *"Conveyed"* is an active transport verb implying the person was taken somewhere
- The contrast with Canonchet ("captured and executed") and Quaiapen ("killed") is stark

### Why the Distinction Matters for Joan

If Anashuecot was **exported** rather than killed, then:
1. The 31-year documentary gap (1672–1703) has a specific explanation: Anashuecot was physically removed from the record-keeping jurisdiction
2. The Absolom affidavit's purpose shifts — it's not a death notice, it's a **land-rights preservation act** by a counsellor documenting the displacement of the legitimate heir
3. The question "could Joan be Anashuecot?" acquires a new branch: if Anashuecot was exported, could she have returned? Or is Joan a different person entirely?
4. If Anashuecot was sold to Bermuda, Barbados, or elsewhere, there might be **arrival records** at the destination

**Per Law 3 (No Premature Disqualification):** All three interpretations are held open. The export interpretation is strongest on current evidence, but none are eliminated.

## Was Removing Sachem-Line Women a Deliberate Strategy?

Southern New England kinship operated through matrilineal or strongly matrilateral principles. Women of sachem families could be:
- Carriers of hereditary legitimacy
- Links between chiefly lineages
- Potential claimants or brokers of land rights
- Mothers of future sachems
- Nodes through which a dispersed community could reconstruct political authority

The documented colonial objectives were usually expressed in immediate terms: punish rebellion, prevent renewed warfare, obtain revenue from captives, remove Indians from contested land, disperse communities.

**Best assessment:** The removal and destruction of Narragansett women in sachem lineages likely weakened hereditary and territorial claims, and colonial officials had practical reasons to understand that effect. However, current evidence supports this as a **structural consequence or probable strategy**, not as a conclusively documented, gender-specific written policy.
