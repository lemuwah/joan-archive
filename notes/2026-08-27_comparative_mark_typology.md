## Why Build This Before We Have Joan's Mark?

We're blocked on accessing Joan's mark directly (see `notes/2026-08-27_joans_mark_search.md`). But we don't need Joan's mark to build the framework it will eventually be placed into.

When we eventually see Joan's mark — whether through a future digitization, an in-person visit, or a change in institutional policy — we want to be able to immediately say:
- "This looks like a standard English X mark" or
- "This is unusual — here's how it compares to other marks from the same period and region" or
- "This resembles pictographic marks from [specific tradition]" (with appropriate cultural framing)

Without a baseline, we'd just be guessing.

## What We're Cataloguing

### Mark Types (Known from Colonial New England Records)

1. **X mark** — The most common. A simple cross or X, sometimes with the person's initials written nearby by the clerk. Used by both literate and illiterate signers.
2. **Initials** — The person writes their initials (e.g., "J G") but not a full signature. Indicates partial literacy.
3. **Full signature** — The person signs their name. Indicates full writing literacy.
4. **Pictographic / symbolic mark** — A non-alphabetic symbol: a drawing, a geometric shape, a personal emblem. These are culturally significant in many traditions.
5. **Witnessed mark** — The clerk writes "her mark" or "his mark" and the person makes a mark (type unspecified in the transcription). This is what we have for Joan.

### Cataloguing Fields Per Mark

For each mark we find in a browsable archive, we record:
- **Person:** Name, gender, role (grantor, grantee, spouse/joinder, witness)
- **Date:** Of the deed or document
- **Location:** Town, colony
- **Mark type:** X, initials, signature, pictographic, or other
- **Mark description:** What does it look like? (if visible in the scan)
- **Context phrase:** Exact wording used in the document ("her mark," "his mark," "signed and sealed")
- **Source:** Archive, volume, page, URL
- **Image available?** Yes/no, and link if yes

## Comparison Groups

Joan's mark should eventually be compared against these groups:

### Group A: English Colonial Women, Rhode Island, 1670s–1690s
The most direct comparison. Women in Joan's social orbit — same colony, same decade, same legal context (dower release, joinder, consent).

### Group B: English Colonial Women, Other New England Colonies, Same Period
Broader comparison to see if Rhode Island practices differed.

### Group C: English Colonial Men, Rhode Island, Same Period
To understand whether mark style varies by gender or is consistent within a community.

### Group D: Indigenous Marks on Colonial Documents (Handled Separately)
This group exists for awareness only. We include it because:
- The Anashuecot hypothesis is an open question in the archive
- If Joan's mark is ever compared to Indigenous marks, that comparison must be made **with full cultural context, not as a casual visual match**
- The Brown "Their Marks" project (Kimberly Toney, John Carter Brown Library) is the authoritative resource here
- We will not catalog Indigenous marks ourselves — that work belongs to Indigenous scholars and communities

**We note with respect that Indigenous pictographic marks carry deep cultural and personal significance. They are not simply "signatures" in the European sense. Any comparison must be made with this understanding.**
