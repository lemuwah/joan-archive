# Greene-Smith Relationship — From Arrival to Political Split
**Date:** 2026-08-27  
**Sources:** John Greene's 1679 affidavit; *Public Records of the Colony of Connecticut* Vols. 1–3; Updike *Richard Smith, First English Settler of the Narragansett Country*; Smith's Castle research publications

## Summary

John Greene of Quidnessett arrived at Cocumscussoc c.1637–42 and "lived with" Richard Smith Sr. He was most likely a household employee or trading agent, not a formal partner. They co-signed the 1663 Connecticut jurisdiction petition but by the 1680s were on opposing sides of the RI/CT dispute. Greene was **not** an Atherton Company member but benefited from its settlements.

---

## Timeline of the Relationship

### Phase 1: Arrival and Household Association (c.1637–1651)

**John Greene's 1679 affidavit** states:
> *"forty years and more, Mr. Richard Smith, that I then lived with, did first begin and make a settlement in the Narragansett."*

**"Lived with"** is the key phrase. It indicates a close household and working relationship but does not specify whether Greene was servant, employee, tenant, or co-investor.

A modern scholarly account describes business being conducted through *"John Greene, a member of his [Smith's] household,"* and hired agents before Smith permanently relocated to Narragansett.

**Best characterization:** Greene was a resident associate of Smith's early Cocumscussoc enterprise, probably functioning as a household employee or trading agent. No partnership agreement, accounting record, or deed establishing equal ownership has been identified.

Greene independently acquired approximately 100 acres in Narragansett in 1651, though he apparently did not build on that property until about 1661.

### Phase 2: Co-Signers on the 1663 Connecticut Petition

Both John Greene and Richard Smith signed the July 3, 1663 petition asking the Narragansett territory to come under Connecticut jurisdiction. At this point they appear aligned — both are Narragansett settlers seeking protection from Rhode Island's assertion of control.

### Phase 3: The Split (1670s–1680s)

By the late 1670s–1680s, Greene and Smith were on **opposing sides:**
- **Richard Smith** remained Connecticut-aligned, serving as Wickford commissioner and Atherton Company proprietor
- **John Greene** acted as a **Rhode Island/Warwick agent** in England (with Randall Holden), arguing that the Narragansett lands had not been legally purchased by the Atherton claimants

In 1671, Greene had to seek remission from Rhode Island for having previously adhered to Connecticut's government.

**Per Law 1 (No Narrative Smoothing):** This political arc — from Smith's household, to co-signer, to political opponent — must be held as a documented trajectory, not smoothed into either "lifelong allies" or "always opposed."

### Phase 4: The Atherton Company Question

The Atherton Company (1659) named the following investors:
- Humphrey Atherton
- John Winthrop Jr.
- Richard Smith Sr.
- Richard Smith Jr.
- William Hudson
- Amos Richardson
- John Tinker

**John Greene does not appear in the surviving list of purchasers.** The deed specifically excluded land previously sold to Richard Smith.

Greene benefited indirectly from the Atherton settlement:
- He lived among settlers occupying the disputed lands
- His own land and livelihood depended on English claims in the area
- He became a leading defender of settlers' possession when Rhode Island challenged their titles

**But** Greene later joined Randall Holden in arguing that the Indians had placed their lands under the King's protection rather than selling them outright — a position that **challenged** the Atherton Company's sweeping claims.

**Best characterization:** Greene was an early Smith-associated settler and later advocate for settlers' possession — not an original Atherton Company land speculator. His political trajectory moved from Connecticut-alignment to Rhode Island-alignment, and he ultimately argued against the legal basis of the Atherton purchases.

## Significance for Joan Research

1. Greene's trajectory from Smith's household employee to independent landholder to political agent mirrors the arc of a man building independence over 40 years. Joan enters the documentary record (1682) during the period when Greene is most independent.
2. Greene's argument against the Atherton purchases does NOT exonerate him from complicity in Native dispossession — he still held Narragansett land. But it means he was not a simple land-grabber.
3. The Smith-Greene split complicates the Seven-Name Network. They co-sign documents and live in the same orbit, but the network is a contested community, not a unified faction.
