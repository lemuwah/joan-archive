## What Anochetuckett Is

**Anochetuckett** (modern form: **Annaquatucket**) is a small river in North Kingstown, Rhode Island, west/southwest of Wickford village, flowing toward Bissel Cove. It appears in two 1687 entries in Bartlett Vol. 3:

1. **Richard Wharton's Royal Patent (29 June 1687)** — uses the boundary *"Annocotuckett's river"*
2. **Coginaquand's Namcock Neck Deed (recorded 4 July 1687, possibly re-recording of 1659 original)** — uses *"Anochetuckett"* as one of several place-names describing the tract

## Etymology

The strongest gloss is approximately **"at the end of the river"** or **"at the end of the tidal current."** The element **-tucket/-tukett** refers to a river, tidal stream, or place associated with a stream. This is a common Algonquian hydronym morpheme (cf. Pawtucket, Nantucket).

## Why It Is NOT Anashuecot

The superficial similarity between **Anochetuckett** and **Anashuecot** comes from shared phonetic elements in English transliteration of Algonquian sounds. Scholarship treats them as distinct:

> *"Their superficial similarity is probably the result of English attempts to represent unfamiliar sounds and should not be used to infer that they were the same name, person, clan, or place."*

- **Anashuecot** = a person, named as *"chiefe Sachem and heire properly"* in the 1672 Fones Purchase deed
- **Anochetuckett** = a river, used as a geographic boundary marker in land deeds
- **-cot** (person) vs. **-tucket** (river/stream) = different Algonquian morphemes

**Per Law 1:** The similarity is documented. The distinction is documented. No collapse is made.

## Geographic Significance

The Annaquatucket River is located within approximately **one mile of Wickford/Cocumscussoc** — Richard Smith's trading post and John Greene's territory. It serves as the **southern boundary** of the Cocumscussoc area in historical reconstructions.

**This means the river runs through Joan's home territory.** It is a geographic feature of the land she lived on, not a record of her name. But it does confirm that Algonquian naming was embedded in the landscape around her home — the land itself carried Indigenous names that English deeds had to record.
