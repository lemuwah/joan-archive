### Finding 2 ("Conveyed" mechanics)
**Weak point:** The claim that "conveyed" had no single legal definition rests on a plausible general point about 17th-century vernacular legal English, but the Explorer does not cite a single period RI statute, court record, or dictionary (e.g., a period law glossary, or Bartlett's Records of the Colony) that directly demonstrates *both* readings ("conveyed out" vs. "conveyed to") in actual Rhode Island usage. The two readings are asserted as a framework, not demonstrated from two contrasting primary instances.

**Attack:** Find the mirror-image proof: at least one 1670s–80s RI document using "conveyed to [named recipient]" for a person (not property), alongside the Absolom affidavit's "conveyed out." Without that pair, this is a linguistically reasonable hypothesis dressed as a settled reading framework.

**Shadow model:** If "conveyed" in this period and register overwhelmingly means *property transfer* rather than *person transfer* when used without "out," then "conveyed out of ye country" in the Absolom affidavit could just as easily be read as "documents/records were removed from the country" rather than "Anashuecot was physically removed" — which would undercut the wartime-displacement reading of a 🟢 VERIFIED anchor line. This shadow model has NOT been ruled out by the current finding.

**Verdict:** The Providence Vol. 15 graduated-service-term schedule is strong, real, independently verifiable evidence — but it proves the disposal *system* existed, not that "conveyed" specifically, as a word, meant what the Explorer says it meant in the one sentence the whole theory leans on.
