## Attack 1: H10 — Pre-War Succession

**The claim:** The Quidnessett succession gap is pre-war (Scuttup d.1668), not war-caused.

**Attack:** This is **chronologically correct but causally empty.** Knowing that Scuttup died in 1668 tells us WHEN the gap opened. It tells us NOTHING about WHO filled it or WHY. Anashuecot appearing in 1672 could be:
- Direct descendant of Scuttup (via a wife we don't know)
- Lateral successor from a parallel sachem family
- A sub-sachem elevated by Quaiapen (still alive in 1668–1672)
- Someone installed by English colonial pressure
- An unrelated figure who seized opportunity

The hypothesis acts as if narrowing the succession window to 1668–1672 is progress. It's not — it's the same unknown gap, just shorter. **The gap between Scuttup and Anashuecot is 4 years, not decades. This actually REDUCES the space in which anything mysterious happened.**

**Verdict:** SURVIVES as a factual correction but DOES NOT generate new evidence. It narrows the timeline without filling it.

## Attack 2: H11 — Awassuocitt = Anashuecot

**The claim:** 1654 witness Awassuocitt = 1672 signatory Anashuecot, based on phonetic similarity.

**Attack:** This is the project's most dangerous hypothesis right now.

**The phonetic case:** Aw-ass-uoci-tt ↔ Aw-ash-ue-cot. Yes, the initial syllable and terminal consonant rhyme. But in a colonial record environment where English scribes spelled Algonquian names by ear, phonetic overlap is **cheap.** How many other names in the same record set look similarly "close" to each other? The project has already acknowledged this problem (proof piece #1: "unreliable spelling doesn't CREATE connections") but then uses unreliable spelling to propose this very connection.

**The William Baker problem DESTROYS the family structure.** If Baker is Awassuocitt's "eldest son" and Baker is documented as English since 1638, then either:
- The deed is misread (Baker is a separate witness, not a son) — in which case we can't trust the family structure at all
- Baker really is Awassuocitt's son — in which case Awassuocitt had an English wife before 1638, making him a thoroughly anglicized figure, which contradicts the "erasure" narrative
- Baker is adopted / has a different status — which we're inventing without evidence

**The project correctly flagged this as an interpretive problem but then kept the hypothesis alive anyway.** If the family structure can't be trusted, the identification can't be trusted either.

**Verdict:** HELD OPEN per Law 3 but DEGRADED to low confidence. The phonetic case is weak, and the Baker problem undermines the supporting evidence.

## Attack 3: H12 — Three Stacked Phonetic Hypotheses

**The claim:** Tomanick = Tocomminon AND Awashousse = Awassuocitt = Anashuecot, so father and son appear together in 1644.

**Attack:** This is **speculation stacking.** Three unproven phonetic identities chained together to produce a conclusion. Each individual link is "plausible but not proven." Three "plausible but not proven" links multiplied together produce something much weaker than any individual link.

The project has been exemplary about flagging this — it says "HIGHLY SPECULATIVE" and "three stacked phonetic hypotheses" and "recorded but not promoted." Good. But the Hostile Reviewer's job is to ask: **should it be recorded at all?**

Recording it creates a gravitational pull. Future analysis will reference "the 1644 counselor connection" as if it were a finding rather than a triple speculation. The project's own Law 4 warns against AI-generated content looping back as source material. This hypothesis IS that loop — it was generated by analytical reasoning, not documentary evidence, and recording it on the repo gives it the appearance of a finding.

**Recommendation:** Keep the 1644 Act of Submission data (Tomanick and Awashousse as Pessicus's counselors). Delete the three-way phonetic chain. Let the data sit. If a FOURTH document independently connects any two of these names, then the chain earns its place.

**Verdict:** DATA PRESERVED, CHAIN DELETED. The individual facts are real. The connection is fabrication-adjacent.

## Attack 4: The Escalating "Special Explanations" Count

**The project claims Model B now requires 8 independent special explanations.**

**Attack:** This is rhetorically effective but methodologically questionable.

Not all 8 are "independent." Several are variations of the same problem:
- #1 (no paper trail), #2 (no Joan in four colonies), #3 (neighbors documented), and #8 (invisible wife of a protector) are all **the same fact restated four ways:** Joan has no English documentation. That's one anomaly, not four.
- #4 (mother clause) and #6 (Female Inheritance Window) are both about the 1682 deed language. Two framings of one observation.

Honestly counted, Model B requires **3–4** special explanations:
1. Joan's total absence from English records (consolidating #1/#2/#3/#8)
2. The "or to her mother" clause (consolidating #4/#6)
3. The survival anomaly (#5)
4. The pre-war succession coincidence (#7)

3–4 is still a lot. But it's not 8.

**The project should recount honestly.** Inflating the number weakens the argument — a hostile academic reviewer would catch it immediately and question everything else.

**Verdict:** VALID CRITIQUE. Recount to 3–4 independent anomalies. The argument is strong enough without inflating.

## Attack 5: Weunquesh's 1679 Court Appearance

**The claim:** Weunquesh exercising land authority in 1679 connects to Joan's 1682 case.

**Attack:** It's a **parallel**, not a connection. Weunquesh is at Westerly. Joan is at Quidnessett. Different locations, different land claims, different legal contexts. The only link is: both are women, both involve land, both are in RI, both are within a few years of each other.

That said, the parallel IS valuable — it proves that female Indigenous land authority **existed and was recognized** in RI courts within 3 years of Joan's deeds. It doesn't prove Joan was Narragansett. It proves the mechanism existed.

**Verdict:** SURVIVES as contextual evidence (the mechanism existed). DOES NOT connect directly to Joan's case.
