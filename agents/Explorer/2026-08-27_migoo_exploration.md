## Hypothesis 1: Joan's Mark Exists in a Digitized Collection We Haven't Browsed Yet

**The idea:** The RI State Archives has digitized Land and Public Notary Records on their Preservica platform. The 1682 deeds may be viewable online already. If so, Joan's actual mark — whatever it looks like — is already accessible.

**Why this might be true:**
- The RI State Archives Preservica platform is confirmed live: https://sosri.access.preservica.com/
- Multiple volumes of Land Evidence have been digitized page by page
- The archive's own neg-space log places the 1682 home-place deed in "Vol. IV" and the Pawtuxet deed in "RI Land Evidence Vol. VII" (Records of RI & Providence Plantations). These reference different numbering systems.
- Worthington's 1921 published abstracts cover Vol. I (1648–1696) but are abstracts only — they would not reproduce marks

**Where evidence would hide:**
- RI State Archives Preservica digital scans — browse page by page for "Greene" in the 1681/82 section
- FamilySearch microfilm 22291–22292 (Peirce Manuscripts of RI Land Evidences)
- FamilySearch catalog item 133744 (North Kingstown Land Evidence Records 1686–1921) — deeds may have been re-recorded after 1686
- Early Records of the Town of Providence, Vol. 14 (deed book) on Internet Archive — for the Pawtuxet/Carpenter deed
- RIHS MSS 1210 (Kettelle Papers), Box 2, Folder 13 — "Greenes of Quidnessett" research papers — may contain photocopies of original deeds

**What contradiction would appear if false:**
- If the 1682 deeds are NOT in any digitized volume, the mark is not yet accessible online. This doesn't kill the lead — it means we need physical archive access.

**If this were true, what would Joan's mark tell us?**
- Is it an "X"? That's the most common mark for an illiterate English person.
- Is it a symbol? Narragansett marks on deeds sometimes use pictographic signatures (see the "Their Marks" project at Brown University / JCB Library for comparators).
- Is it identical to or distinct from the Awawsunks "her marke" on the 1674 deed? The archive already has images of that mark.
- Is it the same hand/ink as John Greene's signature? If Joan's "mark" was added by the scribe rather than by Joan herself, the physical evidence matters.

**Status:** SPECULATIVE — requires browsing Preservica scans to test.

## Hypothesis 2: The "Or to Her Mother If She Survive" Clause Is Structurally Unusual

**The idea:** The 1682 home-place deed reserves a 30-shilling life-estate to Joan — "or to her mother if she survive." This clause names no one. But it proves Joan's mother was alive (or believed to be alive) in March 1682. What does it imply?

**Why this might matter:**
- In standard English colonial deeds, naming "the mother" of a wife in a life-estate provision is uncommon. Dower provisions normally run to the wife, then to the estate, not to the wife's mother.
- If Joan were an English settler woman whose mother lived nearby, the mother would likely appear in other colonial records — church membership, court testimony, land transactions. No such woman has been found.
- If Joan were an Indigenous woman whose mother held kinship authority, naming the mother in a land transaction involving Narragansett territory could be structurally consistent with Narragansett practices — but we cannot assume this without Indigenous scholarly input.

**Guardrail:** Narragansett kinship structures are the domain of Narragansett scholars and the Narragansett Tribal Historic Preservation Office. This hypothesis respects that boundary absolutely. We do not project English legal categories onto Narragansett kinship, and we do not project assumed Narragansett categories onto Joan's record. The clause is an observation, not a conclusion.

**Where evidence would hide:**
- Comparable deeds from the same period where a wife's mother is named — a baseline study of 1680s RI deeds would test whether this clause is truly unusual or just uncommon.
- Narragansett kinship scholarship (e.g., Lisa Brooks, *Our Beloved Kin*; Linford Fisher, *The Indian Great Awakening*) for documented matrilineal land-transfer patterns.

**What contradiction would appear if false:**
- If other English colonial deeds commonly include "or to her mother" clauses, the clause is unremarkable.

**Status:** UNVERIFIED — requires baseline study and Indigenous scholarly input before evaluation.

## Hypothesis 3: Joan's Mark and Awawsunks's Mark Are Comparable Physical Evidence

**The idea:** The archive already holds images of Awawsunks "her marke" from the 1674 deed. If we obtain images of Joan's mark from the 1682 deed, we can do a physical comparison — not to prove identity between the two women, but to establish whether Joan's mark follows English conventions (an "X") or uses a distinctive symbol.

**Why this matters:**
- The archive has already identified that "Absolom his marke" on one document appears to show a bow-and-arrow symbol, which was reclassified from NULL to UNVERIFIED after reviewing the actual scan. Physical marks carry real information.
- Awawsunks is a documented female sachem who signed "her marke" with correct gendered language — the scribe got her gender right. The 1672 Fones deed uses "his marke" for Anashuecot — which may be scribe error or may reflect that the Narragansett language has no grammatical gender (per the archive's linguistic lane).
- Comparing Joan's mark to Awawsunks's mark is not an identity claim. It's a pattern study. Were marks standardized by scribes? Did different people make different marks? Were Indigenous marks visually distinct from English marks?

**Where evidence would hide:**
- The "Their Marks" project at Brown University / JCB Library catalogs Indigenous pictographic signatures on 17th-century deeds.
- RIHS MSS 1210 (Kettelle Papers), Box 2, Folder 13
- RIHS MSS 461 (Henry Whitman Greene Papers) — contains 1680s deeds from Warwick area

**What contradiction would appear if false:**
- If Joan's mark is a standard "X" with no distinguishing features, the comparison tells us nothing about identity — only that she was illiterate (consistent with either English or Indigenous identity).

**Status:** SPECULATIVE — requires obtaining Joan's mark image to test.

## Hypothesis 4: The Pawtuxet Deed Witnesses Connect Joan to the Shawomet Circle

**The idea:** The 19 May 1682 Pawtuxet deed is witnessed by John Wickes and Samuel Gorton — both original 1643 Shawomet purchasers. `contradictions/warwick-pawtuxet-overlap.md` already flags this as a real network overlap (C1). Joan's "free & voluntary consent" was witnessed by two men who operated in both English and Narragansett social networks.

**Why this might matter:**
- Samuel Gorton was a radical separatist whose community at Shawomet (later Warwick) had documented relationships with Narragansett leaders.
- If Joan were connected to the Narragansett sachem line, having Gorton and Wickes witness her consent would be structurally consistent.
- But: Gorton and Wickes might simply be neighbors or legal associates. Proximity does not prove kinship identity.

**What contradiction would appear if false:**
- If Gorton and Wickes witnessed many deeds for many people (as was common for prominent community members), their presence on Joan's deed is unremarkable.

**Status:** SPECULATIVE — requires baseline study of Gorton/Wickes witness patterns.
