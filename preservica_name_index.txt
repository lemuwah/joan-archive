When a little group left Portsmouth in 1639 to found.Newport at the
southern end of Aquidneck, the secretary William Dyre brought with him the
Colony's record book. In this book he continued to note the official acts and
many of th~ first land grants within the Newport bounds. In March 1640/41
Portsmouth and Newport jointly ordered .that each town should have a book for
recording land evidence.
In 1779 the British Army evacuated Newport and too~ with them the records
of the Town. After the ship sank near New York, the records were fished up
and kept at New York for three ye~rs before the remnants were returned to Newport. These were restored, but the earliest land evidence.now_ available commences about 1714~ The Newport Land Evidence, whatever there was between 1640
and 1714, has been lost forever.
Fortunately for us the earliest settlers were delinquent in .recording
their real estate transactions. Refer to volume I of ''Rhode !~land Colonial
Records" by John Russell Bartlett. On page 127 the General Assembly in 1644
outlined the problem and ordered that sales should be proved in writing before
a Court and recoided by the Colony Secretary. On page 177 the revised procedures were incorporated into the 1647 code.of Colony laws.
On pages 195-197 of Bartlett the duties of the Recorder were set ~orth.

The earliest General Assembly minutes and court records were kept in the
"Great Book of Records for the Colony, 11 -now known as "R .J. Colony Records 16461669" (as stated in Chapin, above). Land evidence was inserted by the Colony
Recorders in a rather random manner, wherever there was some vacant space.
The recording of real estate transfers continued to be lax and constituted
a great pioblem. Peopl~ ~ho had been granted land or whp had bought land had~□
moved away, abandoning their property (and sometimes leaving a stack of unpaid
bills). Land had been sold and resold with no official records of the transactions. Squatters had moved onto property and had not "been molested. Land
owners had encroached upon adjacent roads and commons and had even fenced off
and completely bloc~ed public roads. Affairs were in such a mess in 1662 that
a new law was enacted:
Court of Election held at Warwick, May 22d, 1662
rourt of Commissioners held the same day:
First Act of Limitation
An act passed the court requiring all persons having or
pretending to have any claim to the title of lands in the
•
possession of others, who hold and enjoy the same as their own,
to commence an a~tion for the land so claimed by them, on or
before the 1st of June, ~663, or be debared after that time,
and the lands to be recorded as the estates of the persons in
possession, their heirs and assigns. Claimants living out of
the colony, not more than 200 miles distant were allowed one
year more, and claimants in England~ or foreign part~ biond sea;
were allowed two years more to sue for their land, and no more.
- "Memoir of Rhode Island" by Henry Bull IV
under thapter dated "1662" •
This act caused a -few people to record their claims, but it becomes evident that not all sales made the record.
In 1671 the "Great Book of Records for the Colony~ seems to have been
filled. Recorder John Sanford then commenced a new bo6k which is now called,
"R.I. Land Evidence, Volume I, 164B-1696". The title.is slightly in error,
because the book in~ludes deeds as far back as 1642 although not recorded until
three decades later. General Assembly minutes and court records. were kept
elsewhere.
The title is also misleading when it seems to restrict the contents to
land evidence. In addition to deeds and land grants, there are also a few
wills, protests against the elements by mariners, some receipts, a couple of
estate inventorie~, and even a highway report.
Even the "Hhode Island" is misleading because the records apply to Fall
River, the banks of the Taunton River, Dartmouth, Massachusetts, the Elizabeth
Islands, and who knows where else. (A few documents concern land back in Old
England.) It should be kept in mind that in the ancient days Tiverton and
Little Compton were part of Plymouth Colony, not Rhode Island.
We Newporters and Middletowners should not complain but should be grateful
that these duplicate(?) records were preserved, incomplete though they may be.
Three more volumes, called "RILE volumes II, III,&. IV'!, are preserved in
the R.I. Archives, 5tate House, Providence. The amount of land evidence tapers
off, with the space taken up more and more by documents of other sorts. These
books are followed by four others, "Public Notary Records" through 17B4. I
have examined the contents of these latter only hurriedly; if there is any
land evidence, it is not conspicuous. In fact, RILE-IV is probably a complete
misnomer and should have been labeled "Public Notary Records" also. To assist
historians, all eight volumes ought to be indexed in several ways. Reliable
abstracts like the printed volume of RILE-I would be appreciated.

Any books as f~ll of names as these Land Evidences and Notary Records

This index to RILE-I lists an estimated 130D people; because of the bad
spelling, it is difficult for me to determine how many duplicates there are.
The.documents contain genealogical information, occupations, political appointments, military ranks, weather conditions, as well as real estate descriptions. From the direct information plainly stated, all sorts of things
can be deduced, and all sorts of questions can be raised. (Richard Baily,
Richard Barnes, George Hamande, John Heath, (Rev.) William Hiscox, John Hulme,
John Jerson~ Robert Little, Henry & Joseph Timberlake, Elias Williams, and
John Winchco~be only appear in RILE-I as witnesses; how come they were so
often available as witn~sses?) The information has b~en in those documents
for several centuiies, but good indexes are required to make the information
readily available.
To be truly useful, before printing the documents should ha~e been grouped
geographically and then arranged chronologically within each group. For my
own purposes I have done part of that job elsewhere for RILE-I; all docume~ts
pertaining to Newport-Middletown were copied and bound in chronological order.
Properly presented, otherwise dull deeds, mortgages, and wills provide an
interesting and more understandable story of flowing history. Abstracting
(omitting all the repetitious legal !garbage"} in the pri~ted RILE-I did half
the job.
This present index to RILE-I is elementary. Every mention of a person's
name is referenced to the page number of the 6riginal book. The original page
number was used to avoid confusion with page numbers of the several modern
copies. (!ncidently, what happened to pages 280 to 289 of RILE-I? They're
missing from the printed version at least. To compensate, there are two pages
each numbered 84, 85, 86, and 87. If I had realized this fact during my
indexing, the pag~s would have been designated 84A, 84B, BSA, 85B, etc. But
I didn't, and they weren't, and now it's confusing. Next time ••..• )
I deem it regretable that the names of the Recorders were omitted
throughout the printed version of RILE-I.
It suited my fancy and needs to identify each reference with the person's
importance to that particular document: granter, grantee, witness, recorder,
abutter to property, attorney, etc. See the list of abbreviations for my
definitions. Some references got no identifying label. If Quentin Smith
were mentioned as the father of Robin Smith to tell the world which Robin
Smith was meant, ~uentin got no ldentifier - just a page reference. A granter
or grantee label generally indicates that a lot of information pertaining to
that person's affairs may be available • . A witness label generally says that
a certain person was at a certain place at a certain time and capable of signing his name or making his mark. Of course, to some people even that mite of
information could be valuable.
The poor spelling and/or poor interpretation of ancient handwriting makes
indexing of the names difficult. The variations of spelling stretch the
combinations and permutations to their limits. I have had some experience
with odd spellings, but I don't know what should be accepted as a moder~-day
standard. Hicks - Robinson - Westcott - Wilcox - four surnames that are
severely distorted, but how many · of the variations have survived? Offhand I
don't know. It would be nice if some better-informed person(s) revised this
index by adding several hundred cross-references. After three centuries, the
Indians deserve some justice - it was rare when their names were spelled
twice the same way.
