## The Question

Who signed the 1672 Fones Purchase deed, what were their relationships to each other, and does the signatory structure tell us anything about Joan's identity?

---

## Six Anomalies in the Signatory Pattern

⚠️ *Flagged per Law 4 — these observations are analytical, not evidence.*

1. **Single kin group.** All six grantors are from one family — a chief sachem, his two brothers, and his three sons. This is not a multi-party transaction between unrelated sachems; it's a family disposing of family-held land.

2. **"Chiefe Sachem and heire properly."** This phrasing is unusual in English deeds of the period. "Heire properly" suggests hereditary right, not purchased or granted title. Standard English deed language would say "owner" or "proprietor."

3. **Three sons sign.** Including sons in a land transaction suggests succession planning — the land is being conveyed by the family that holds hereditary right, and the next generation consents.

4. **Absolome as witness, not grantor.** Absolome was a counsellor to sachem Moseupp — a different sachem. His presence as witness (not party) suggests inter-sachem diplomatic protocol, not just a neighbor watching a sale.

5. **No English co-sellers.** Unlike many Narragansett land transactions of this period, the 1672 deed has no English intermediary or co-seller. The land passes directly from Indigenous hands.

6. **Gender classification.** The deed uses male kinship terms — "brothers" and "sons" — for the co-grantors. Anashuecot is not explicitly gendered in the deed text. The clerk's default was male; this is noted but cannot be independently verified or discounted from the surviving document.

## Earlier Sachem Network — Scuttup & Quequaquenuit Generation

The Fones Record contains earlier deeds involving a preceding generation of Narragansett sachems:

| Name | Alternate spellings | Title | Relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scuttup** | Scuttop | "Chiefe Sagamore or Sachem of Naragansets" | Brother of Quequaquenuit |
| **Quequaquenuit** | Quequaganut, Wequachanuit, Gideon | "Chiefe Sagamore or Sachem of Naragansets" | Brother of Scuttup |

- In a 1660 instrument, Scuttup signed on behalf of Quequaquenuit "by his order"
- Both are described with the same sachem title used for Anashuecot
- The relationship between this brother-pair and Anashuecot's family group is **not documented in the Fones Record** — possible predecessors, parallel sachems, or kinsmen, but no explicit genealogical link

## Awashonks Clarification

**Awashonks** (Awashunkes) was a genuine female sachem (*saunkskwa*), but she was chief of the **Sakonnet** people — a Wampanoag-related community in the Little Compton area, **not** a Narragansett sachem.

- She is **NOT** a signatory to the 1672 Fones Purchase deed
- The phonetic similarity between "Awashonks" and "Anashuecot" is noted but **is not evidence of identity** — these are different names from different communities
- Awashonks is well-documented independently; conflating her with Anashuecot would be contamination

## Model Assessment

### Model A — Joan = Anashuecot

**Argument:** The 1672 deed shows a single Narragansett kin group — Anashuecot + brothers + sons — disposing of hereditary land at Quidnessett. Ten years later, Joan signs the 1682 deed for a homeplace on the same land, alongside her husband John Greene. If Joan is Anashuecot, the land continuity is explained: it stays in the same family.

**Strength:** The kin-group structure (chief sachem + brothers + sons) is consistent with Narragansett hereditary land tenure. The "heire properly" language supports hereditary right. The same land appears in both deeds.

**Weakness:** No document explicitly states Joan = Anashuecot. The connection is inferred from land continuity and naming patterns, not from a direct identification.

### Model B — Joan = Unknown English Woman

**Argument:** John Greene acquired land through the Fones Purchase as one of six English purchasers. He then married an English woman named Joan, whose origins are simply unrecorded (common for colonial women). The 1682 deed reflects a standard English property transaction.

**Strength:** Many colonial women are undocumented. The absence of Joan's origins is not unusual by itself.

**Weakness:** Model B must explain:
- Why Joan has no English paper trail at all (no maiden name, no family, no parish, no ship)
- Why the 1682 homeplace sits on the same land the Anashuecot kin group conveyed in 1672
- Why John Greene's relationship with Narragansett leadership persists across both deeds
- How John acquired uncontested possession of land that four jurisdictions were competing to control

---

## Null Hypothesis Test

**Search: Any English woman named Joan connected to the Fones Purchase?**

Result: **No.** The Fones Record contains no English woman named Joan as a party, witness, landowner, or other connected person in any Fones Purchase transaction. Other English women appear in neighboring conveyances (e.g., Anne Denison, who relinquished dower rights in a 1672 deed), but none was named Joan and none was a party to the Fones Purchase.
