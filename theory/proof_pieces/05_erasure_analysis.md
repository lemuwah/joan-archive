## The Question

Joan appears in exactly **two legal documents**, both in 1682. She has no documented existence before or after these two signatures. What can we conclude from these appearances — and what can't we?

---

## The Two 1682 Deeds

### Deed 1: Home-Place Division (March 24, 1681/82)
- John Greene divides 180 acres: 120 to Daniel, 60 to James
- Life-estate clause: sons pay 30 shillings annually "as long as their father or mother should live" / "or to her mother if she survive"
- Joan is **named** but does not independently sign this deed
- Source: F.L. Greene (1894, p. 10); North Kingstown Land Evidence

### Deed 2: Pawtuxet Sale Consent (May 19, 1682)
- Sale of Pawtuxet land to William Carpenter for £10
- Joan gives **"free & voluntary consent"** and signs with **"her marke"**
- Source: Records of the Colony of RI & PP, Vol. VII, pp. 177–178

## Multi-Angle Stress Test

### Assumption 1: "Joan's consent was unusual — it proves special legal status"

**CHALLENGED. Joan's participation was standard practice.**

Research into Rhode Island land records 1670–1690 reveals **at least 23 separate transactions** where wives participated as grantors, consenting spouses, or dower releasors. Examples:

- **Horrud Porter** (1671): separately released "all my Right intrest and Title… Notwithstandinge my jointure or Dower"
- **Anne Coddington** (1672): joined as co-grantor
- **Hannah Haviland** (1675): explicitly released her "Right of Dowry thirds"
- **Joan Brightman** (1676/77): declared she gave her "consent unto the Deed"
- **Martha Simmons** (1680): gave her "asent" to the sale
- **Mary Almy** (1681): "consent[ed] to…sale"
- **Rebeckah Browning** (1687/88): gave her "free consent"

Source: *Rhode Island Land Evidences* (Worth, 1921 — Internet Archive)

**Conclusion:** Joan's "free & voluntary consent" in the Pawtuxet deed is **routine dower-release language**, not evidence of unusual legal standing. A prudent purchaser like William Carpenter would have sought exactly this release to protect against future dower claims. This is how English land law worked.

⚠️ **Impact on Model A:** This removes one potential argument — Joan's legal participation is NOT evidence of special Indigenous authority. It's standard English wife practice.

⚠️ **Impact on Model B:** This SUPPORTS Model B — Joan is behaving exactly like every other English colonial wife in the surviving records.

---

### Assumption 2: "'Free and voluntary' is special phrasing indicating Indigenous consent protocol"

**CHALLENGED. This is standard legal boilerplate.**

"Free and voluntary" was the conventional formula for dower releases across the English colonies. A 1762 Maryland form illustrates the typical template:

> *"being by me privately examined a Part from and out of the hearing of her said Husband did release … all her right of Dower … and she did also declare that she made this release and acknowledgement voluntarily and freely and without being induced thereto by fears or Threats of or ill usage by her said Husband or fear of his Displeasure"*

The purpose was to establish:
1. The wife knew what she was relinquishing
2. Her consent was independent, not coerced by her husband
3. Statutory acknowledgment requirements were met
4. The purchaser received clean title

Source: Maryland State Archives, colonial conveyancing forms

**Conclusion:** The phrasing does not indicate anything unusual about Joan's legal status.

---

### Assumption 3: "Joan signed with a mark because she was Indigenous and illiterate"

**CHALLENGED. Marks were universal among colonial women.**

The surviving Rhode Island land-evidence abstracts document **at least 13 women** who explicitly signed with marks in this period — including Mary Manchester, Martha Simmons, Ann Makepeace, Sarah Waite, Anne Brayton, and others. **Zero women** in these abstracts have confirmed handwritten signatures.

Note: Mary Williams of Providence used a mark on a 1661 legal document despite evidence she could read and write — the mark was a legal convention, not necessarily a literacy indicator.

Source: *Rhode Island Land Evidences* (Worth, 1921)

**Conclusion:** Joan's mark does not distinguish her from English-born colonial women. It does not indicate Indigenous origin.

---

### Assumption 4: "Joan's total absence before 1682 proves she was hidden/erased"

**PARTIALLY CHALLENGED. But not fully explained by Model B either.**

**For Model B (normal):** Many colonial women appear only in land records. The surviving record set for 17th-century Rhode Island is sparse. Women were not independently documented in most administrative contexts. Joan's absence before 1682 is **not unusual** in isolation.

**For Model A (erasure):** Joan's absence is **deeper** than typical:
- No maiden name — not even in the deed itself
- No family of origin documented in any colony
- No marriage record (church or civil)
- No ship manifest or immigration record
- No church membership or admission
- No pre-1682 legal appearance under ANY name
- John Greene was legally and politically active from ~1639 — his wife is invisible for 40+ years

**The comparison that matters:** Other Quidnessett wives ARE documented before their land-deed appearances. The question is not "is Joan's absence unusual for any random colonial woman" but "is it unusual for the wife of a politically active landowner in this specific community?"

### Assumption 5: "Joan had legal authority over Pawtuxet land too — she wasn't limited to Quidnessett"

**CORRECT but MISFRAMED.**

Joan's consent on the Pawtuxet deed is a dower release — she's **releasing** a claim on land her husband is selling, not **exercising** authority over it. Under English common law, dower attached to all real property the husband held during the marriage. When he sold Pawtuxet land, Joan's potential dower interest in that land needed to be released.

This is significant because:
- It proves Joan was **legally recognized** by colony magistrates in 1682
- It proves she was **alive and present** in May 1682
- It proves she was accepted as **John Greene's wife** by the legal system
- It does NOT prove she had independent authority, Indigenous or otherwise

---

### Assumption 6: "Narragansett women had independent land authority that would have made Joan's consent different"

**TRUE IN NARRAGANSETT CONTEXT, BUT NOT VISIBLE IN THESE DEEDS.**

Narragansett women — especially female sachems like Quaiapen ("the Old Queen") — held genuine political and territorial authority. Quaiapen was a Narragansett female sachem and landholder in her own right.

However:
- English colonists often refused to recognize Indigenous women's authority unless expressed through a deed signed by a male sachem
- The 1682 deeds show Joan operating within **English legal forms** — dower release, consent, mark — not within Indigenous land-tenure protocols
- If Joan was Anashuecot, she was using English legal mechanisms, not Narragansett ones
- The deeds do not betray Indigenous legal concepts — they are standard English conveyancing

**Conclusion:** Model A's argument cannot rest on the legal form of Joan's 1682 appearances. If she was Anashuecot, the erasure was complete enough that the English legal system processed her as an English wife.
