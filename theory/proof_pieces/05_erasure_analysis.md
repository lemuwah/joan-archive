## Part 2: The "Or to Her Mother If She Survive" Clause

> **⚠️ WORDING UNVERIFIED — not in F.L. Greene 1894 or the Worth 1921 abstract; see contradictions/mother-clause-wording.md.** F.L. Greene 1894 p. 10 (verbatim): *"each of said sons to pay thirty shillings annually as long as their father or mother should live"* — "their mother" is Joan. Worth 1921 abstract of the James deed: *"...unto John Green and after his decease to Joane Greene his wife."* Neither names Joan's mother. Part 2 below was written on the "her mother" reading and is preserved unaltered (Law 1); its conclusion is **conditional** on that reading being confirmed in the North Kingstown manuscript (FamilySearch DGS 008204949, img 10). If the manuscript matches the abstract, this Part collapses and Model B is *not* weakened by it.

### Previous Assumption: Standard English Dower Language

**WRONG.**

Research confirms this clause is **NOT standard English dower language**. It is a conditional survivorship provision — a gift that passes to the mother if she outlives the primary beneficiary.

### Standard Dower Language (for comparison)

- "with her free consent" — married woman's consent
- "relinquishment of all her right of dower and thirds" — dower release
- Wife signing and separately acknowledging the deed — joinder and authentication

### "Or to her mother if she survive" — NOT Dower

- No parallel clause using these exact words was found in any Massachusetts, Connecticut, or Plymouth deed series from 1660–1700
- The phrase is a **conditional remainder** — it creates a fallback beneficiary (the mother) if Joan predeceases the life-estate period
- In standard English practice, if the wife died before the husband, dower simply never vested. The property would pass to the heirs without complication. There was no need to name the wife's mother as a fallback

### Why This Matters

**Under English common law, a wife's MOTHER has no legal interest in her son-in-law's property.** The only reason to name Joan's mother as a contingent beneficiary is if:

1. Joan's mother had some recognized claim or relationship to the land itself, OR
2. The family was operating under a **different inheritance logic** where the maternal line carried land rights

**Model A interpretation:** Under Narragansett matrilineal inheritance, land passes through the female line. If Joan = Anashuecot (or a woman in Anashuecot's matrilineal kin group), her mother would have a recognized claim to the land through Indigenous inheritance customs. The clause is John Greene encoding Narragansett inheritance norms into English legal form.

**Model B interpretation:** Model B must explain why John Greene — a man deeply familiar with English property law after 40+ years of colonial litigation — inserted a clause that has no English legal parallel and makes no sense under English inheritance rules. An English wife's mother has no dower, no curtesy, no claim. The clause is legally anomalous.

## Part 3: The Joinder Paradox

### The Question You Asked

*Why would the record need her consent when she didn't exist on paper before 1682?*

### The Legal Answer

Under English common law, a wife's **dower** (contingent future claim to one-third of her husband's real property) existed automatically by virtue of marriage — no prior documentation was required. A purchaser wanted the wife to join the deed and release that claim to protect against a future widow's action.

**Joan's joinder was a conveyancing device.** It did not create her legal existence. It is simply the first surviving evidence that she was John Greene's wife.

### Why This Answer Is Incomplete

The legal mechanism explains WHY consent was sought. It does NOT explain:

1. **Why Joan has zero prior documentation.** Dower exists automatically, yes — but actual colonial wives leave OTHER traces: baptisms, court appearances as witnesses, family Bible entries, church rolls, town meeting records, estate inventories. Joan has NONE of these.

2. **Why she appears in 1682 specifically.** The Female Inheritance Window (1676–1682) coincides with the post-King Philip's War period when:
   - Male Narragansett sachem lines were broken (Canonchet executed 1676)
   - Indigenous persons needed certificates to move freely (Oct 1676 RI order)
   - Indigenous women married to English men had both legal danger and legal opportunity
   - The 1682 deeds may represent the moment when recording Joan's existence became legally necessary for land protection

3. **Why "or to her mother if she survive" was included.** This has no English legal justification. English dower did not extend to the wife's mother.

## Part 4: Indigenous Women Under English Names

### Search Result

No fully documented case from 1670–1700 Rhode Island proves all three criteria: (a) an Indigenous woman, (b) married to an English man, (c) recorded under an English name in a surviving colonial legal document.

### Best Candidate: Elizabeth Gasesett / "Minnetinka" (wife of John Cory)

- John Cory's probate names "wife Elizabeth"
- A 1713 deed identifies "Elizabeth, widow of John Cory" conveying land
- A 1904 genealogical account states Cory was "said, but not proved" to have married a Narragansett chief's daughter
- The documents do NOT identify her as Indigenous or give the name Gasesett

**Assessment:** This is evidence for the **possibility** that Indigenous women appeared under English names in colonial records. It is not proof meeting the full standard. But the pattern exists: an Indigenous woman, married to an English man, appears in English legal documents under an English name only, with her Indigenous identity surviving only in genealogical tradition.

⚠️ **Per Law 4:** The Elizabeth Gasesett case is a comparator, not a proof. It demonstrates the mechanism by which Joan's Indigenous identity could have been overwritten — but it does not independently prove Joan was Indigenous.
